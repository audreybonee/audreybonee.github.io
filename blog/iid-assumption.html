The Model That Cried 200 OK: Trading I.I.D. Ideals for Production Reality

A practical look at what happens when production data stops behaving like training data, and why healthy APIs can still serve deeply unhealthy predictions.

December 15, 2025 · 9 min read

“In theory, there is no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is.” — Yogi Berra

Machine learning is built on a simple promise: if the future looks like the past, we can predict it.

To make this work, most models are trained under a quiet assumption—one so fundamental that textbooks introduce it on page three and then politely forget it ever existed. It is the assumption that data points are Independent and Identically Distributed (I.I.D.).

It is a beautiful mathematical cornerstone. But I.I.D. is not a law of nature; it is a convenience. It is also one of the first assumptions that production systems silently violate, usually right after you have closed your laptop for the weekend. And when that happens, the real danger is not that the model crashes. The danger is that it keeps running.

The Pristine World of I.I.D.

When we invoke I.I.D., we are making two massive claims: that our data points are independent—one row does not influence the next—and identically distributed—past, present, and future data all originate from the same probability distribution, $P(X, Y)$.

This assumption is why we trust validation sets. It promises that a model succeeding in the lab will naturally succeed in the wild.

But production is not a lab. Production is traffic spikes, broken upstream pipelines, economic shocks, UI redesigns, and whatever happened on TikTok this week. As Chip Huyen points out, real-world data is non-stationary by default. The $P(X, Y)$ you trained on yesterday is already evolving today.

I.I.D. is a laboratory baseline—a controlled condition that lets us train a model on Monday and hope the world holds still until Friday. When the world shifts, our monitoring hits a blind spot. Traditional software throws errors. ML just keeps serving predictions—wrong ones.

Software Fails Loudly, ML Fails Quietly

This is where ML engineering parts ways with traditional software engineering.

When a traditional service breaks, it tends to break loudly. A null pointer exception. A failed health check. A 500 propagating up the stack. PagerDuty wakes someone up. The on-call engineer reads a stack trace, locates line 45, and ships a fix. The system tells you it is broken. The system screams.

Machine learning systems do not do this.

When the input distribution drifts, your model does not raise an exception. It does not return a NaN. It does not log a warning. It returns a perfectly well-formed prediction—a float between 0 and 1, a class label, a recommendation—with high confidence and zero indication that anything has gone wrong. The API returns 200 OK. The dashboards stay a comforting, verdant green. The model is working, in the narrow sense that tensors are flowing through it and numbers are coming out the other side—much like a GPS is technically working when it routes you into a lake.

This is what Rabanser and colleagues at Amazon were getting at in their paper Failing Loudly: An Empirical Study of Methods for Detecting Dataset Shift. Their thesis is that ML systems, left to their own devices, fail quietly—and that we need explicit machinery to make them fail loudly, the way the rest of our infrastructure does. Without that machinery, model degradation surfaces not as a crash, but as a slow, mysterious decay in business metrics—quiet enough to evade engineering, yet expensive enough to eventually summon finance.

It helps to have a taxonomy here, because distribution shift comes in several flavors:

Covariate shift

$P(X)$ changes but $P(Y|X)$ stays the same. The inputs we see have shifted, but the underlying relationship between inputs and outputs has not. A credit model trained mostly on urban applicants now sees a flood of rural ones—the population changed, but the risk relationship did not.

Label shift

The mirror image, where $P(Y)$ changes while $P(X|Y)$ holds. The base rate of fraud doubles, even though fraudulent transactions still look the same as before.

Concept drift

The nasty one, where $P(Y|X)$ itself changes. The same input now means something different. A user who clicked five articles in a session used to be highly engaged; post-redesign, that is average behavior. The features did not change. Their meaning did.

These shifts demand different responses. But all of them share the same failure mode: silent, confident, wrong.

Engineering for a Moving World

So what do we actually do about it?

The first move is to stop treating training as the finish line. As Chip Huyen points out, training is merely the beginning of a model’s lifecycle. Most of the engineering work happens after deployment: monitoring, alerting, retraining, rollback.

Code rusts slowly, but models rot immediately. In traditional software, you write a function once and it works until the requirements change. In ML, you train a model once, and it begins degrading the second it hits production.

A useful starting point is to monitor inputs, not just outputs. Waiting for accuracy to drop is too late, because accuracy requires ground-truth labels, which often arrive days or weeks after the prediction. By the time offline evaluation tells you the model has degraded, the damage is done.

Instead, we monitor the distribution of the features going into the model in real time. If today’s traffic looks meaningfully different from the training data, we have our early warning. To measure this, the industry relies heavily on two metrics: Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and the Population Stability Index (PSI).

KL Divergence: The Information-Theoretic View

It calculates how much information you lose when you try to describe today’s messy reality using yesterday’s assumptions. If we have our training data distribution ($P$) and our current production data ($Q$), the formula looks like this:

$$D_{KL}(P \| Q) = \sum_i P(i) \log\frac{P(i)}{Q(i)}$$

The intuition here is elegant: if production matches training exactly, every term becomes $P(i) \times \log(1) = 0$, and the divergence is zero. There is no surprise. But the logarithm makes this metric hyper-sensitive to disappearances. If a feature value was common in training ($P$ is high) but vanishes in production ($Q$ is tiny), the metric spikes. It is specifically designed to catch that “we used to see this regularly and now we do not” signal.

There are just two catches. First, KL is asymmetric—measuring from $P$ to $Q$ is not the same as $Q$ to $P$. Second, if $Q(i)$ hits absolute zero, the math explodes. In production, you either patch this by adding a tiny epsilon to every bin, or you use its more robust cousin.

PSI: The Pragmatic Cousin

If KL Divergence is the academic theorist, PSI is the grizzled industry veteran. It is essentially a symmetrized, crash-proof version of KL divergence:

$$\text{PSI} = \sum_i (P_i - Q_i) \log\frac{P_i}{Q_i}$$

Born out of the high-stakes world of credit risk modeling, PSI does not just give you a number; it gives you a playbook. Over decades, it has hardened into an industry standard with clear rules of thumb:

  • Below 0.1The distribution is stable. You can sleep soundly.
  • 0.1 to 0.25Yellow flag. Something is shifting.
  • Above 0.25Red flag. The population has fundamentally changed.

These thresholds are not laws of nature. They are heuristics inherited from decades of credit scoring. But they give teams a shared vocabulary, which matters far more than mathematical purity when you are trying to convince an incident responder that a shifting distribution is actually an emergency.

A Minimal Implementation

Here is a minimal implementation, adapted from patterns teams at Evidently AI and Arize AI have written about extensively: 

import numpy as np

def calculate_psi(reference: np.ndarray, current: np.ndarray,
                  bins: int = 10, epsilon: float = 1e-4) -> float:
    """
    Population Stability Index between a reference and current distribution.
    """
    # Build bin edges from the reference distribution's quantiles
    quantiles = np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)
    bin_edges = np.quantile(reference, quantiles)
    bin_edges[0], bin_edges[-1] = -np.inf, np.inf  # absorb tail values

    ref_counts, _ = np.histogram(reference, bins=bin_edges)
    cur_counts, _ = np.histogram(current, bins=bin_edges)

    # Convert to probabilities, smoothing zeros to avoid log(0)
    ref_pct = ref_counts / len(reference) + epsilon
    cur_pct = cur_counts / len(current) + epsilon

    psi_per_bin = (ref_pct - cur_pct) * np.log(ref_pct / cur_pct)
    return float(np.sum(psi_per_bin))


def calculate_kl_divergence(reference: np.ndarray, current: np.ndarray,
                            bins: int = 10, epsilon: float = 1e-4) -> float:
    """
    KL divergence D_KL(reference || current), binned the same way.
    """
    quantiles = np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1)
    bin_edges = np.quantile(reference, quantiles)
    bin_edges[0], bin_edges[-1] = -np.inf, np.inf

    ref_counts, _ = np.histogram(reference, bins=bin_edges)
    cur_counts, _ = np.histogram(current, bins=bin_edges)

    p = ref_counts / len(reference) + epsilon
    q = cur_counts / len(current) + epsilon

    return float(np.sum(p * np.log(p / q)))

A few details that tripped me up the first time I implemented this, which rarely show up in tutorials:

  • Bin from the reference, score the current. Define your bin edges once, from training data, and reuse them forever. If you re-quantile every batch, you are measuring how the bins shifted, not how the data shifted. It is a subtle bug that can quietly invalidate months of monitoring.
  • Smooth your zeros. That epsilon is not optional. Without it, the first time production sees a value outside the training range, your metric returns infinity and your alerting system loses its mind.
  • Compute per feature, not on the prediction alone. Monitoring only the output distribution is necessary but not sufficient. By the time the output shifts, the cause is already upstream. PSI on each input feature gives you a triangulation that helps with root cause analysis.
  • Pick a sensible window. A 24-hour rolling window is typical for high-volume systems; a 7-day window for slower ones. Too short, and you will alert on every weekday-versus-weekend cycle. Too long, and you will miss real shifts until they are catastrophic.

Trading Silence for Visibility

A monitoring layer built on PSI and KL divergence does not fix distribution shift. Nothing fixes distribution shift, because the world insists on changing whether your model is ready or not. What it buys you is visibility—the ability to see a problem when it starts, instead of when it has already cost you a quarter of revenue.

That is the same trade traditional software engineers made decades ago when they built logging frameworks and metrics dashboards. ML engineering is still, in many organizations, where backend engineering was in 2005: heroically deployed, optimistically monitored, and one bad week away from a public incident.

The teams I have seen handle this well treat their feature distributions the way SREs treat latency percentiles. They define SLOs on PSI. They have runbooks for what to do when a feature exceeds 0.25. They retrain on cadence and on signal, and they are explicit about which one they are doing and why. The point is to make their models fail loudly—because the alternative is letting models keep producing confident, beautifully typed predictions while quietly being wrong.

In theory, the training and production distributions are the same. In practice, the world drifts, users change, incentives evolve, and data moves underneath us. The goal of ML engineering is not to pretend this instability does not exist, but to build systems resilient enough to survive it. As Yogi Berra warned us from the very beginning: in theory, there is no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is.
